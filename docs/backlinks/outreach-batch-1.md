>
> Most people who keep checking for a reply aren't really waiting for a text. They're waiting for relief from not knowing. - **Use for requests like:** "How to get over fear of rejection," "asking someone out," "job-search rejection," "putting yourself out there."

> Hi [Name],
>
> Fear of rejection gets smaller when rejection gets more specific. Write down the exact event you're afraid of ("they say no to a second date") and, separately, the meaning you've attached to it ("I'm undesirable"). The event might happen. The meaning is an interpretation, and keeping the two apart stops one no from becoming a verdict on who you are.
>
> Then practise on purpose with a "rejection ladder": ten small asks rated from mildly to very uncomfortable. Start around a 3 out of 10, such as asking a stranger a question or sharing a preference, and only move up once it feels ordinary. Judge each attempt by what you controlled (did I ask clearly?), not by the answer. And keep one recovery sentence ready for when a no lands: "I'm disappointed, and I can handle disappointment."

**Title:** Stop Waiting to Feel Confident. Run a 10-Minute Experiment Instead.

> Most of us treat confidence like a permission slip. We wait to feel ready, then we'll speak up in the meeting, send the message, sign up for the class. The feeling rarely arrives on schedule, so the action rarely happens.
>
> Try flipping the order. Confidence isn't the thing that comes before action. It's what builds up after you've acted a few times and noticed you survived. Believing you can handle something and having handled it are two different kinds of proof, and only the second tends to stick.
>
> Here's a simple way to collect that proof on purpose.
>
> **Why your doubts never get corrected**
>
> Anxiety about a situation is usually a prediction in disguise: *this will go badly. People will notice. I'll freeze.* The trouble is we rarely test those predictions. We avoid the situation, nothing bad happens, and the prediction survives untouched, ready to stop us again next time.
>
> A confidence experiment is just a way to test one prediction deliberately.
>
> **The four steps**
>
> **1. Predict.** Before you act, write one plain sentence about what you expect: "If I ask a question in the team call, I'll stumble and people will think I wasn't paying attention." Make it specific enough to be proven wrong.
>
> **2. Act.** Do the small thing. Keep it small, a three or four out of ten on the discomfort scale, not a ten. You're collecting data, not proving you're fearless.
>
> **3. Observe.** Afterwards, write down only what actually happened, the way a camera would have recorded it. "I asked the question. Two people nodded. The manager said 'good point' and answered it." Leave out your interpretation of their faces.
>
> **4. Record.** Compare the prediction with the observation, and keep the page. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that matters. A single good experience fades by Friday. A written record of twenty of them is hard to argue with.
>
> **Five small experiments to start with**
>
> - **Speak early.** In your next meeting, contribute in the first ten minutes instead of waiting until you've rehearsed the perfect point.
> - **Initiate.** Send the invitation instead of waiting to be invited.
> - **Ask the basic question** you'd normally edit out because you worry it sounds obvious.
> - **Submit something before it feels ready:** the draft, the application, the post.
> - **Ask directly for what you want**, in one sentence, without a paragraph of justification first.
>
> Do one per day for a week. Each takes minutes. The goal isn't for every experiment to go well. Some won't, and those count too. What you're really learning is that the outcome you predicted was usually more dramatic than what actually happened, and that when things did go a little wrong, you handled it.
>
> **What about affirmations?**
>
> They're fine as preparation. Rehearsing what you'll say, or reminding yourself of a strength you forget under pressure, can help you walk in the door. Just don't let preparation replace the experiment itself. You can't affirm your way to evidence you haven't collected.
>
> **The record is the point**
>
> Six weeks from now, the doubtful voice will still show up before the next hard conversation. That's normal. The difference is that you'll have a notebook, or a note on your phone, full of predictions that didn't come true. Confidence stops being a feeling you hope for and becomes something you can look up.
>
> Start with one prediction today. Make it small, test it, and write down what really happened.
